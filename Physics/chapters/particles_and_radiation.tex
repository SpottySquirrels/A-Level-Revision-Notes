\section{The Atom}
    An \gls{atom} is made up of a \gls{nucleus} (a core of \glspl{proton} and \glspl{neutron}) surrounded by \glspl{electron_shell}.
    This \gls{nucleus} makes up most of the mass of the \gls{atom}, but is very small compared to the \glspl{electron_shell} around it.
    \Glspl{electron} are in \glspl{electron_shell} around the \gls{nucleus}, representing different energy values of the \glspl{electron}.

    \Glspl{proton} are positively charged and \gls{electron} are negatively charged, therefore, for \glspl{atom} to be neutral, there must be an equal number of \glspl{proton} and \gls{electron}.
    The mass of an \gls{atom} is given (in relative atomic mass units) by the number of \glspl{proton} plus the number of \glspl{neutron}, since the mass of \gls{electron} are negligable at this scale.
    An atom of element X can be written as its relative atomic mass (written A or $A_r$) and atomic number (written Z) (the number of \glspl{proton}).
    From its mass and atomic number, the number of \glspl{neutron} can be found by subtracting the number of \glspl{proton} from the total mass.
    The number of \glspl{electron} must be the same as the number of \glspl{proton}, since atoms are neutrally charged.
    The notation for the \gls{atom} in this form is:
    \begin{equation}
        \ce{_Z^AX}
    \end{equation}
    For example, uranium-235 (a uranium \gls{atom} (element number 92, symbol U) with a relative atomic mass of 235) would be written as:
    \begin{equation}
        \ce{^{235}_{92}U}
    \end{equation}

\section{Radioactive Decay}
    \subsection{Alpha Decay} \label{alpha_decay}
        \Gls{alpha_decay} is where an unstable \gls{nucleus} has to lose mass to become stable, so it releases an \gls{alpha_particle} ($\ce{^2_4{\alphaparticle}}$), which is a particle made of 2 \gls{proton} and 2 \gls{neutron}, equivalent to a helium-2 \gls{nucleus} ($\ce{^4_2He^{2+}}$).
        This reduces the mass of the original \gls{atom} by 4, and it loses 2 \glspl{proton}, changing its element.
        It will also leave the \gls{atom} as an \gls{ion}, which is called ionisation.
        Since the \gls{atom} is losing 2 \glspl{proton}, the \gls{atom} will have its atomic number decreased by 2.

        This is the equation for \gls{alpha_decay} of element X to element Y:
        \begin{equation}
            \ce{_Z^AX -> _{Z-4}^{A-2}Y + ^4_2{\alphaparticle}}
        \end{equation}
        This can also be written with the \gls{alpha_particle} explicitly written as a helium \gls{nucleus}:
        \begin{equation}
            \ce{_Z^AX -> _{Z-4}^{A-2}Y + ^4_2He}
        \end{equation}
        An example of this is the \gls{alpha_decay} of uranium-238 to thorium-234:
        \begin{equation}
            \ce{_92^235U -> _90^231Th + ^4_2{\alphaparticle}}
        \end{equation}

    \subsection{Beta Minus Decay} \label{beta_minus}
        \Gls{beta_minus_decay} is where a \gls{neutron} turns into a \gls{proton}, releasing an \gls{electron} (also called a \gls{beta_minus_particle}) and an \glslink{antineutrino}{electron antineutrino}.
        Inside the decaying \gls{neutron}, a \glslink{quark}{down quark} becomes an \glslink{quark}{up quark}, which releases the \gls{electron} to conserve charge and the \glslink{antineutrino}{electron antineutrino} to conserve lepton/electron number ($\leptonnumber[e]$).
        Since the \gls{atom} gains a \gls{proton} (and thus +1 charge), it becomes an \gls{ion}, which is called ionisation.
        However, whereas \gls{alpha_decay} decreases the atomic number by 2, \gls{beta_minus_decay} increases it by 1.

        The equation for \gls{beta_minus_decay} of element X to element Y is:
        \begin{equation}
            \ce{_Z^AX -> _Z^{A+1}Y + _{-1}^0{\betaparticle[-]} + {\antineutrino[e]}}
        \end{equation}
        This can also be written with the \glslink{beta_minus_particle}{beta particle} explicity written as an \gls{electron}:
        \begin{equation}
            \ce{_Z^AX -> _{Z+1}^AY + {\electron[-]} + {\antineutrino[e]}}
        \end{equation}
        An example of \gls{beta_minus_decay} is the \gls{beta_minus_decay} of hydrogen-3 to helium-3:
        \begin{equation}
            \ce{_1^3H -> _2^3He + _{-1}^0{\betaparticle[-]} + {\antineutrino[e]}}
        \end{equation}

        The force responsible for \gls{beta_minus_decay} is the weak nuclear force, mediated by the $\wboson[-]$ boson.

        Since a \gls{neutron} is turning into a \gls{proton}, the equation for \gls{beta_minus_decay} can be written using the \gls{quark} flavour of the \gls{neutron} and \gls{proton}, as:
        \begin{equation}
            \ce{{\baryonquarks[udd]} -> {\baryonquarks[uud]} + {\betaparticle[-]} + {\antineutrino[e]}}
        \end{equation}
        Which can be further simplified by removing the $\upquark$ and $\downquark$ that are left unchanged, to just leave:
        \begin{equation}
            \ce{{\downquark} -> {\upquark} + {\betaparticle[-]} + {\antineutrino[e]}}
        \end{equation}

    \subsection{Beta Plus Decay} \label{beta_plus}
        \Gls{beta_plus_decay} is very similar to \gls{beta_minus_decay}, however instead of a \gls{neutron} becoming a \gls{proton}, a \gls{proton} becomes a \gls{neutron}, releasing a \gls{positron} (also called a \gls{beta_plus_particle}), and an electron neutrino.
        Inside the decaying \gls{proton}, an up quark becomes a down quark, which releases the \gls{positron} to conserve the charge and the electron neutrino to conserve lepton/electron number ($\leptonnumber[e]$).
        Since the atom loses a \gls{proton} (and thus -1 charge), it becomes an \gls{ion}, which is called ionisation.
        However, whereas \gls{alpha_decay} decreases the atomic number by 2, and \gls{beta_minus_decay} increases it by 1, \gls{beta_plus_decay} decreases it by 1.
        The equation for \gls{beta_plus_decay} of element X to element Y is:
        \begin{equation}
            \ce{_Z^AX -> _Z^{A-1}Y + _{1}^0{\betaparticle[+]} + {\neutrino[e]}}
        \end{equation}
        This can also be written with the \glslink{beta_plus_particle}{beta particle} explicity written as an \gls{positron}:
        \begin{equation}
            \ce{_Z^AX -> _{Z-1}^AY + {\electron[+]} + {\neutrino[e]}}
        \end{equation}
        An example of this is the \gls{beta_plus_decay} of hydrogen-3 to helium-3:
        \begin{equation}
            \ce{_{12}^{23}Mg -> _{11}^{23}He + _{1}^0{\betaparticle[+]} + {\neutrino[e]}}
        \end{equation}

        The force responsible for \gls{beta_plus_decay} is the weak nuclear force, mediated by the $\wboson[+]$ boson.

        Since a \gls{proton} is turning into a \gls{neutron}, the equation for \gls{beta_plus_decay} can be written using the quark flavour of the \gls{proton} and \gls{neutron}, as:
        \begin{equation}
            \ce{{\baryonquarks[uud]} -> {\baryonquarks[udd]} + {\betaparticle[+]} + {\neutrino[e]}}
        \end{equation}
        Which can be further simplified by removing the $\upquark$ and $\downquark$ that are left unchanged, to just leave:
        \begin{equation}
            \ce{{\upquark} -> {\downquark} + {\betaparticle[+]} + {\neutrino[e]}}
        \end{equation}

\section{Feynman Diagrams} \label{feynman_diagrams}
    A Feynman diagram represents an interaction between particles.
    It has a time axis, showing the progression of the interaction from the incoming particles to the outgoing particles.
    
    This is a simple Feynman diagram, representing two electrons ($\electron[-]$) repelling each other.
    The interaction is mediated by the electromagnetic force, carried by a virtual photon ($\photon$):

    \begin{figure} \label{fig:electron_repulsion}

                \begin{feynman}
                    \vertex (i1) {e$^-$};
                    \vertex [above=1.5 of i1] (i2) {e$^-$};
                    
                    \vertex [right=2 of i1] (f1) {e$^-$};
                    \vertex [above=1.5 of f1] (f2) {e$^-$};

                    \vertex at ($(i1) + (.5,.75)$) (a);
                    \vertex at ($(f1) + (-.5,.75)$) (b);

                        (a) -- [fermion] (i2),
                        (f1) -- [fermion] (b),
                        (b) -- [fermion] (f2),
                    };
                    \draw[-stealth] (-.4,-.1) -- (-.4,1.35); % time axis

    A virtual photon is a type of virtual particle.
    Virtual particles cannot be detected, but are put into models such as Feynman diagrams to show force carriers such as photons and W bosons.
    Any `interal' particles (particles that don't enter/leave the diagram) are virtual in Feynman diagrams.

    In Feynman diagrams, fermions (leptons and baryons) are shown with solid straight lines with arrows representing their direction of travel through time.
    Matter travels forwards though time, and antimatter is modelled as travelling backwards, due to its charge being reversed from that of regular matter.
    Some diagrams show antimatter as travelling forwards in time along with regular matter.

    Most bosons (photons, $\wboson[\pm]$ bosons, $\zboson$ bosons) are shown with wavy lines (see the photon in figure \ref{fig:electron_repulsion} and the $\wboson[-]$ boson in \ref{fig:beta_minus_decay}).
    
    Mesons can be shown with a quark and antiquark line, or as a dashed line.

    \section{Leptons}
        Leptons are fundamental particles that do not feel the strong nuclear force.
        The symbol for a lepton is $\lepton$.
        There are three generations of lepton, and each generation has a charged lepton and a neutrino variant.

        The first generation has electrons ($\electron[-]$) and electron neutrinos ($\neutrino[e]$).

        The second generation has muons ($\muon[-]$) and muon neutrinos ($\neutrino[\mu]$).

        The third generation has taus (also called tauons; $\taulepton[-]$) and tau neutrinos ($\neutrino[\tau]$).

        The charged leptons all have a charge of -1, meaning that the charged antileptons all have charges of +1 ($\electron[+]$, $\muon[+]$, $\taulepton[+]$).
        Antielectrons are called positrons.
        The neutrinos are uncharged, so antineutrinos ($\antineutrino[]$) are also uncharged.

        \subsection{Lepton Number}
            Lepton number is a quantum number describing whether or not a particle is a lepton.
            All regular matter leptons (not antileptons) have a lepton number of $\leptonnumber[]=+1$, and all antileptons have a lepton number of $\leptonnumber[]=-1$.
            All non-leptons have a lepton number of $\leptonnumber[]=0$.

            Each generation of lepton has its own lepton number variant; electrons and electron neutrinos (generation I leptons) have an electron number of $\leptonnumber[e]=+1$; muons and muon neutrinos (generation II leptons) have a muon number of $\leptonnumber[\mu]=+1$ and tau and tau neutrinos (generation III leptons) have a tau number of $\leptonnumber[\tau]=+1$.
            All lepton numbers are conserved in interactions, meaning that something such as $\ce{{\neutrino[e]} + {\neutron} -> {\proton} + {\electron[-]}}$ is valid ($\leptonnumber[e]:1+0=0+1$), but $\ce{{\neutrino[e]} + {\neutron} -> {\antiproton} + {\electron[+]}}$ cannot happen, due to electron number ($\leptonnumber[e]$) not being conserved ($\leptonnumber[e]:1+0\neq0-1$).
        
    \section{Quarks}
        There are six types of quark, across three generations.
        The first generation contains up and down quarks ($\upquark$, $\downquark$), the second generation contains strange and charm quarks ($\strangequark$, $\charmquark$), and the third generation contains top and bottom quarks ($\topquark$, $\bottomquark$).
        Each generation gets progressively heavier.
        All quarks either have a charge of $Q=+\third$ (up-type), or a charge of $Q=-\twothird$ (down-type).
        All quarks have a baryon number of $B=+\third$, and all antiquarks have a baryon number of $B=-\third$.

    \section{Mesons}
        See section \ref{zoo:mesons}.

        Mesons are made up of a quark and an antiquark ($\mesonquarks[q][q]$), and take the sum of the quantum numbers from the quarks, for example a meson containing a strange quark and an up antiquark ($\mesonquarks[s][u]\equiv\kaon[-]$) has a strangeness of $S=-1$ from the strange quark.
        
        \subsubsection{Pions}
            Pions are made of two generation I quarks ($\upquark$ and $\downquark$).
            There are three types of pion:
            \begin{itemize}
                \item $\pion[+]:\quad\mesonquarks[u][d]$
                \item $\pion[-]:\quad\mesonquarks[d][u]$
                \item $\pion[0]:\quad\overroot[2](\mesonquarks[u][u]-\mesonquarks[d][d])$
            \end{itemize}
            They represent the four different combinations two of up and down quarks, with the neutral pion ($\pion[0]$) covering the up quark/up antiquark and down quark/down antiquark combinations.
            The neutral pion is in a superposition of $\mesonquarks[u][u]$ and $\mesonquarks[d][d]$, with each state being equally probable\footnote{For some superposition $\psi=\alpha\ket{x}+\beta\ket{y},\;a,b\in\mathbb{C}$, $P(x)=|\alpha|^2\equiv\alpha\alpha^*$ and $P(y)=|\beta|^2\equiv\beta\beta^*$, so for the neutral pion, $P(\mesonquarks[u][u])=\lvert\overroot[2]\rvert^2=\half$, which is the same as $P(\mesonquarks[d][d])=\lvert-\overroot[2]\rvert^2=\half$, thus both states are equally probable.}.
            Due to the neutral pion always being a quark and its own antiquark, it almost immediately decays into two photons ($\ce{{\pion[0]} -> 2{\photon}}$):

        \subsubsection{Kaons}
            Kaons are mesons made from a generation I quark and a strange quark.
            There are four types of kaon:
            \begin{itemize}
                \item $\kaon[+]:\quad\mesonquarks[u][s]$
                \item $\kaon[-]:\quad\mesonquarks[s][u]$
                \item $\kaon[0]:\quad\mesonquarks[d][s]$
                \item $\antikaon[0]:\quad\mesonquarks[s][d]$
            \end{itemize}

    \section{Baryons}
        See section \ref{zoo:baryons}.

        A baryon is a particle made up of three quarks ($\baryonquarks[qqq]$).
        Since all quarks have a baryon number of $B=+\third$, a baryon has a baryon number of $B=\third+\third+\third=1$, since the quantum numbers from each quark are added.
        All baryons have unique antibaryons ($\antibaryonquarks[qqq]$), for example, an antiproton is $\antibaryonquarks[uud]$.
        All antibaryons have a baryon number of $B=-1$, since all antiquarks have a baryon number of $B=-\third$.
        There are multiple different categories of baryons: N, $\deltabaryon$, $\lambdabaryon$, $\sigmabaryon$, $\xibaryon$, $\omegabaryon$.

        \subsection{Nucleons}
            Nucleons are a type of baryon made up of generation I quarks.
            There are two types of nucleon:
            \begin{itemize}
                \item Proton, $\proton:\quad\baryonquarks[uud]$
                \item Neutron, $\neutron:\quad\baryonquarks[udd]$
            \end{itemize}

            Protons have a charge of $Q=+1$, and neutrons have a charge of $Q=0$.
            Protons are the only stable baryon -- every other baryon eventually decays into a proton; energy conservation laws and the Pauli exclusion principle stop neutrons in atoms from decaying\footnote{If a neutron in a nucleus were to decay to a proton, the proton would not be able to occupy low-energy states, since they are all taken by other protons and the Pauli exclusion principle forbids multiple protons in a nucleus from occupying the same energy state, so it would have to occupy a higher-energy state, which would require more energy than staying as a neutron, so conservation of energy forbids it. This is why neutrons only decay inside atomic nuclei (beta minus decay) if there are a lot more neutrons than protons, allowing there to be a lower-energy state for the neutron to decay into.}.

        \subsection{Sigma Baryons}
            Sigma baryons ($\sigmabaryon$) are another type of baryon.
            Sigma baryons contain two generation I quarks and a generation II or III quark.
            Ignoring charmed, top and bottom quark variants, there are three types of sigma baryon:
            \begin{itemize}
                \item $\sigmabaryon[+]:\quad\baryonquarks[uus]$
                \item $\sigmabaryon[-]:\quad\baryonquarks[uds]$
                \item $\sigmabaryon[0]:\quad\baryonquarks[dds]$
            \end{itemize}
            The variants for non-strange quarks are written as $\sigmabaryon[+/-/0][\charmquark/\topquark/\bottomquark]$, and have the same quark composition of the strange sigma baryons, but with the strange quark swapped for a charm quark/top quark/bottom quark.
