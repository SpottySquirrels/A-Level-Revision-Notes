\subsection{Constant Acceleration} \label{constant_acceleration}
    When modelling objects with constant acceleration, (i.e. objects with a constant force being applied and with constant mass), there is a set of equations that relate the object's displacement, initial velocity, final velocity, acceleration, and the time period over which its velocity changes.
    These equations are called the SUVAT equations.

    The first equation is $s=\half(u+v)t$.
    \begin{proof}
        Drawing a velocity-time graph of an object gives a linear function, since $\frac{dv}{dt}=a$, which is constant, so the slope must be constant.
        Thus, the area under it ($s$, since $s=\int v\,dt$) is a trapezium.
        Given that the area of a trapezium is $A=\half(a+b)h$, where $a$ and $b$ are the lengths of the two sides and $h$ is the distance between them, we can find that $s$ is $\half(u+v)t$, since the two sides (the left and right of the graph, at time 0 and $t$) are $u$ and $v$, since the velocity at time 0 is the initial velocity $u$ and the velocity at time $t$ is the final velocity $v$.
        The distance between the two velocities is $t$, thus giving the final formula $s=\half(u+v)t$.
    \end{proof}

    The second formula is $v=at+u$.
    \begin{proof}
        Given that:
        \begin{equation}
            v=\int a\,dt
        \end{equation}
        We can integrate $a$ (given that $a$ is constant) to get:
        \begin{equation*}
            \begin{aligned}
                v=at+C
            \end{aligned}
        \end{equation*}
        Here, $C$ is the value of $v$ at $t=0$, which is the initial velocity $u$, thus:
        \begin{equation}
            v=at+u
        \end{equation}
    \end{proof}

    From these two equations, the other 3 can be derived.
    \begin{itemize}
        \item $s=ut+\half at^2$
        \begin{proof}
            We will substitute the equation $v=at+u$ into $s=\half(u+v)t$.
            This gives:
            \begin{equation*}
                \begin{aligned}
                    s&=\half(u+at+u)t \\
                    &=\half(2u+at)t \\
                    &=ut+\half at^2
                \end{aligned}
            \end{equation*}
        \end{proof}
        \item $s=vt-\half at^2$
        \begin{proof}
            We will rearrange $v=at+u$ to $u=v-at$ and substitute it into $s=\half(u+v)t$ to get:
            \begin{equation*}
                \begin{aligned}
                    s&=\half(v-at+v)t \\
                    &=\half(2v-at)t \\
                    &=v-\half at^2
                \end{aligned}
            \end{equation*}
        \end{proof}
        \item $v^2=u^2+2as$
        \begin{proof}
            We will first rearrange $s=\half(u+v)t$ into $s=\left(\frac{u+v}{2}\right)t$ and $v=u+at$ into $t=\left(\frac{v-u}{a}\right)$.
            Substituting the second one into the first gives:
            \begin{equation}
                s=\left(\frac{u+v}{2}\right)\left(\frac{v-u}{a}\right)
            \end{equation}
            This creates the factored form of a difference of two squares in the numerator, which simplifies to $v^2-u^2$.
            The denominators multiply to get $2a$.
            Rearranging then gives:
            \begin{equation*}
                \begin{aligned}
                    s&=\frac{v^2-u^2}{2a} \\
                    2as&=v^2-u^2 \\
                    v^2&=u^2+2as
                \end{aligned}
            \end{equation*}
        \end{proof}
    \end{itemize}

    If an object is in free-fall, its motion can be modelled with constant acceleration, taking its acceleration to be $g=9.8\,\text{m}\,\text{s}^{-2}$ downwards, which is (approximately) the acceleration due to gravity on Earth at sea-level.
